Case Study 01
PD

Staff Technology Rollout

Blended training for adult learners

380+
users I support
3
layers of support: live, on demand, in person
↓
repeat support requests

The challenge

When my school rolled out a new platform, staff comfort with technology ranged from confident to anxious, and nobody had much time for training. A single PD session would not have stuck.

What I designed

  • A live professional development session where staff practiced the core tasks hands-on.
  • A narrated walkthrough recording they could return to at the moment they needed it.
  • In-person follow-up in classrooms to catch problems the session and the video missed.

Why it works

Adults retain what they practice on their own real tasks, and a one-time session fades fast. Layering live practice, on-demand reference, and in-person support meant each staff member could get help in the form that fit them. Repeat support requests dropped as staff became self-sufficient.

What I'd do differently

The walkthrough was recorded quickly and casually. Today I would script it and break it into short, task-based clips so staff could jump straight to the step they needed.

IT Study Hub

The challenge

People studying for CompTIA certifications face dense exam objectives and a confusing map of which certification to take next.

What I built

A free study site of certification roadmaps, study guides, cheat sheets, plain-language explainers for concepts like Kerberos and VLANs, and a set of procedural guides written for teachers. I plan the structure and topics and use AI to help draft and revise.

What the data showed

  • Human readers spend the most time on compact reference material. Three cheat sheets drew about a quarter of all traffic over 90 days, and the Security+ cheat sheet held readers for nearly two minutes on average.
  • Decision content earns the most reach. When I broke citations down by page, the certification roadmap ("which cert should I get first?") was by far the most-cited page in Microsoft Copilot answers.

What changed because of it

I added study-resource recommendations to the ten highest-traffic pages and moved existing ones higher up the page, so they sit where learners actually read instead of only at the bottom of low-traffic pages.

Case Study 03

CertFlash

Spaced repetition by design, for iPhone and iPad

1,109
flashcards
7
decks aligned to CompTIA exams
SM-2
spaced repetition algorithm

The challenge

Certification exams require retaining hundreds of facts. Rereading notes feels productive but does not build long-term recall.

What I designed

  • A study app built on the SM-2 spaced repetition algorithm, so each card returns right before the learner is likely to forget it.
  • Seven decks aligned to CompTIA exams: A+, Network+, Security+, CySA+, Linux+, Cloud+, and PenTest+. The A+ deck has 209 cards; the six Pro decks have 150 each.
  • A completely free A+ deck, so learners experience the method before deciding to buy.
  • Decks revised as exams change. The CySA+ deck was updated for the new CS0-004 exam.

How the content was made

The card content was drafted with AI. My work was the learning design: the review system, how decks map to each exam, and how the app moves a learner from a first session to steady practice.

What's next

Tagging cards to CompTIA exam objectives with a per-domain progress breakdown, and an exam-date countdown that paces a learner's study.

Walkthrough

A 38-second run through the learner's first session: onboarding that explains the Again / Hard / Good / Easy ratings, the free A+ deck, four cards rated at different confidence levels (each rating shows when the card will come back), and the progress screen that closes the loop.

Case Study 04

PrepPeriod

Designing with users, for iPhone and Apple Watch

2
taps to capture an event on Apple Watch
24
realistic notes used to test the AI feature
Live
on the App Store

The challenge

Teachers are expected to document behavior, parent contacts, and hall passes, but the moments that need documenting happen in the middle of teaching, when there is no time to write.

What I designed

Co-designed with a classroom teacher, who defined the feature set and drove key design decisions. The core model: capture an event in two taps on Apple Watch during class, then complete the details later during a prep period, with reminders timed to the teacher's prep slots.

Iterating on feedback

After launch, users told us they couldn't find how to add students. I added a clear add button to the Students screen and a recovery path for students who had been left without a class.

Testing AI before trusting it

I tested Apple's on-device AI model against 24 realistic classroom notes. Parent-email drafting invented commitments and times, and outcome suggestions gave confident wrong answers for a permanent student record, so I cut both. I shipped only the narrow piece that tested reliably: pre-filling how a parent was contacted. Accuracy comes before automation.

Case Study 05
LMS

Troubleshooting Like a Pro

A short Canvas course for new help desk technicians

3
modules built around one realistic support ticket
15
practice questions with feedback on every answer
AA
built to WCAG 2.1 AA

The challenge

New technicians tend to jump straight to a fix. The six-step troubleshooting methodology CompTIA publishes for A+ gives them a process, but as a bare list of steps it's easy to memorize and hard to apply.

What I designed

  • One realistic ticket, a user who lost her connection after a desk move, carried through all three modules so every step is applied to the same problem.
  • A consistent structure in every module: an overview with measurable objectives, a lesson, and a practice knowledge check.
  • An alignment table connecting each objective to where it's taught and how it's assessed.
  • A final assignment where learners document a new ticket, submitted in writing or as an audio or video recording.

Accessibility

Headings in order, alt text on every image, tables with header cells and captions, the process diagram also written out as text, and contrast-checked colors. Every page passes the Canvas accessibility checker.

How it was made

I used AI tools to draft the content and create the banner, buttons, and diagram, then reviewed it and built and configured everything in Canvas, including the modules, quizzes, and assignment.
